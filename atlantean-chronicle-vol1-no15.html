Six Months Without Hormuz, Washington Books a Century of Venezuelan Crude

On the six-month mark of a war that closed the world's most important oil chokepoint, President Trump announced a Venezuelan concession covering 65 billion barrels across 17 fields — 100-year development rights, a 55% share of output, and the right to buy at cost. Iran spent the same week insisting it controls a strait the Pentagon says it does not.
By The Chronicle International Desk · August 28–30, 2026

The strategic answer to a closed Strait of Hormuz arrived Friday, and it came from the Western Hemisphere. President Trump announced that the United States had entered an agreement giving it access to Venezuela's 65 billion barrels of proven reserves, calling it "the biggest oil deal in world history" and saying it comes at no cost to American taxpayers. Under the structure described by the administration, the United States partners with a private operator to form a new company granted 100-year rights to develop 17 fields; Washington takes an ownership stake amounting to 55% of effective output, plus the right to purchase crude at cost. The deal was negotiated by Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth with Venezuelan leader Delcy Rodríguez, and officials project it could draw $100 billion of investment into Venezuela's oil industry and generate more than $209 billion in taxes for Caracas over its life.

The timing is the story. Friday, August 28 marked six months to the day since the war began, and the Strait of Hormuz — which carried roughly a fifth of the world's oil before February — has still not reopened. Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said this week that Tehran and Oman had reached an understanding on vessel movement and navigation routes, then made clear that an understanding is not a reopening: implementation, he said, depends on Washington honoring the Islamabad memorandum, easing sanctions and paying war reparations. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps navy declared its control of the strait "completely decisive" and called American statements to the contrary "an obvious lie" intended to manage the oil price. US Central Command answered that "Iran does not control the Strait of Hormuz," describing it as an international waterway, and reported having redirected 82 commercial vessels under its blockade of Iranian ports.

Beneath the rhetoric, the numbers have settled into a grinding equilibrium. Brent closed the week at $94.39 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate at $87.06 — well below the $100-plus panic of late July, and well above anything the global economy had budgeted for. Analysts estimate the war has removed roughly 8 million barrels a day of the roughly 20 million that once moved through Hormuz. The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre still rates the threat level in the strait as severe. The United Nations maritime agency reports 19 seafarers killed since February 28 and more than 70 attacks on international shipping in the Persian Gulf; at least 6,000 sailors remain aboard hundreds of ships trapped inside it.

Pressure is now the instrument of choice on both sides. Treasury's Operation Economic Outcast moved this week against branches of Banque Misr in the UAE, proposing to revoke correspondent banking access over roughly $1.8 billion processed for 103 companies with potential ties to Iranian shadow-banking networks between January 2024 and June 2026. Iranian exports are down nearly 35% under the combined weight of sanctions and the naval blockade. Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei publicly acknowledged the country's "economic and livelihood challenges, such as inflation, unemployment" — an unusual admission — while Iranian officials signaled openness to renewed talks with Washington on terms of respect rather than pressure.

Read together, the week describes a change in how this conflict is being fought. The military phase has become an economic siege with a maritime perimeter, and the American response to a supply shock in the Gulf is a century-long claim on supply in the Caribbean. For operators, the practical implication is that the energy premium of 2026 is being treated by policymakers as structural rather than temporary — which is exactly how the prediction markets are now pricing it in the Oracle below, and exactly what the Travel desk is seeing in fuel-driven schedule cuts heading into Labor Day.

A Glacier Gave Way Above the Trishuli: 750 Dead, 3,000 Missing, and 589 Foreign Nationals Unaccounted For

The Chronicle International Desk · August 26–30, 2026

On the morning of August 26, a suspected glacier collapse near Langtang Lirung in the Himalayas sent flash floods and mudslides down a 45-mile stretch of the Trishuli River, destroying dozens of settlements across Nepal's Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading, Gorkha, Tanahun, Nawalparasi and Chitwan districts. The collapse itself registered as a magnitude 5.2 tremor on seismometers worldwide.

By Sunday, official tallies from Nepal and China put the confirmed dead at 750 with more than 3,000 people still missing. Nepal's disaster agency counted 734 dead and at least 2,498 missing within its borders, including 589 foreign nationals; Chinese authorities confirmed at least 16 dead in Tibet with 546 missing. Reporting earlier in the week placed roughly 90 Americans among the missing.

The search is now in its fifth day, which is the point at which recovery operations usually displace rescue. For a country whose late-summer economy depends on trekking season, the corridor destroyed is also the corridor most heavily traveled by foreign visitors — the reason the missing-persons list spans so many nationalities. Travelers with Nepal itineraries booked for September should expect route closures and should confirm status through their operator and their embassy before departure.

The attrition problem behind the headlines: more than a dozen US military, diplomatic and intelligence sites across the region have been damaged since February, and the interceptors used to defend them are the scarcest item in the inventory.

Warsh Turns September Into a Coin Flip — and the Question Is Still a Hike, Not a Cut

The Chronicle National Desk · August 24–28, 2026

Chair Kevin Warsh used his Jackson Hole keynote on Friday to say that this summer's better-than-expected inflation readings do not tell him underlying trends have meaningfully improved, and that he remains committed to fighting inflation. The market read it exactly as intended. CME FedWatch moved to a roughly 56% probability of a quarter-point increase at the September 15–16 FOMC meeting; Kalshi put it at about 48% and Polymarket at about 49%. Before the speech, the odds of no change were close to 70%.

That is the second consecutive Chronicle cycle in which the live question at the Fed has been whether rates go up. The July 29 meeting, which this paper flagged as genuinely contested, ended in a divided hold. September carries a Summary of Economic Projections and a fresh dot plot, which raises the stakes on a decision that is now, on the numbers, a coin flip. The energy shock is doing the work: gasoline at a $4.14 national average and diesel near record August highs are inflation inputs that no amount of patience resolves.

The Interceptor Math: Four-Fifths of THAAD Gone

The Chronicle National Desk · August 17–29, 2026

Six months of war has consumed the munitions that matter most. Reporting through August indicates the US military has expended nearly four-fifths of its THAAD inventory and roughly half of its Patriot interceptors since February. Center for Strategic and International Studies researchers estimate fewer than 827 Patriot and fewer than 278 THAAD interceptors remain, against pre-war stocks of about 2,200 modernized Patriots and 452 THAAD rounds. Analysts say replacement takes years, not months, and that thinner magazines force commanders to accept more risk — firing fewer interceptors at incoming drones and missiles, and raising the odds one gets through. Officials have publicly pushed back on the most alarming characterizations while urging faster production.

Thirty Days to a Shutdown, Zero Appropriations Bills Enacted

The Chronicle National Desk · August 28–30, 2026

The House returns Tuesday, September 1 with a single unavoidable item on the calendar. Fiscal year 2026 ends September 30 and Congress has not enacted a single appropriations bill funding any federal agency for fiscal 2027, which means a continuing resolution is the only mechanism keeping the government open on October 1. The Senate has passed a stopgap reported to run through December 11, paired with language delaying implementation of new federal grant regulations. Thirty calendar days remain, and the House schedule contains far fewer than 30 session days.

Also this week: Nvidia reported the largest quarter in its history — $96.2 billion in revenue, up 106% year over year, with $89.0 billion of it from the data center segment (see Technology). Tropical Storm Dolly formed Thursday as the fourth named storm of a remarkably quiet Atlantic season and had degenerated back into an open wave by Friday morning. And Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell Music sued Anthropic in federal court in Northern California over AI training data, days before the company's expected public IPO filing.

The domestic through-line is the same as the international one: energy costs are now the binding constraint on policy. A Fed that would rather be cutting is debating a hike, a Congress that has not funded fiscal 2027 has to price a war it has not declared into a stopgap, and a consumer paying $4.14 for gasoline is the reason both problems are hard.

Hall County Slams the Door on Data Centers — Unanimously, and With Detention Centers and Dense Housing Attached

Hall County Board of Commissioners · August 27, 2026

Commissioners voted unanimously Thursday, August 27 to impose new moratoriums covering three of the most contested land-use categories in the county at once: data centers, detention centers, and high-density housing rezoning applications. The data center pause extends a freeze that was set to expire this summer, and it buys time for a proposed Data Center Ordinance that would formally define and regulate the facilities in unincorporated Hall County — setting siting standards meant to protect nearby communities, infrastructure capacity and the environment.

The vote puts Hall in the middle of a statewide movement rather than out in front of one. Roughly one in five Georgia counties now has a data center ordinance or is drafting one, with 34 counties and 23 cities having adopted moratoriums or ordinances or begun drafting them; Savannah has been weighing its own amid concerns along the coast. Readers of No. 14 will recognize the pressure source: OpenAI's confirmed 1,400-acre, 3.2-gigawatt Project Camellia campus in Effingham County drew packed and hostile community meetings, and every county in the state with transmission capacity is now doing the arithmetic in public.

A Millage Increase, a Planning Commission Returning, and an Interim Director

Hall County Government · August 11–27, 2026

The same Thursday meeting approved a millage rate increase for the 2026 tax year — the revenue side of a year in which the county has been buying land for a downtown courthouse and staffing up its development review. Commissioners also confirmed that the Hall County Planning Commission will officially return beginning in November, restoring a formal review layer between applicants and the board at precisely the moment three categories of application are frozen.

On the administrative side, Captain Barry Shaw of the Hall County Marshal's Office was named interim director of Development Services following the August 11 resignation of Development Services Director Kristie Turner. The sequencing is worth noting for anyone with a project in the pipeline: a new interim director, a returning planning commission, a millage increase, and a moratorium on the three hottest use classes — all inside one month. Expect the fall calendar to be procedural.

The Most Expensive Labor Day in Years — and the First One With Fewer Passengers

The Chronicle Travel Desk · August 24–30, 2026

What happened: The holiday weekend arrives with the fuel shock fully priced in. The airline trade group expects 16 million paying passengers on US carriers between August 27 and September 3, down 5.7% from 17 million in the same period last year — a rare decline in a holiday forecast. Jet fuel averaged $160.47 a barrel from June 1 to August 12, up 79% year over year. AAA puts average domestic round-trip airfare near $750 with domestic hotel bookings up 9% from last year. On the ground, the national average for gasoline was $4.14 on August 27 and diesel averaged about $5.40 in mid-August, both records for the season; GasBuddy has warned the average could run to $4.80 through Labor Day if the Strait of Hormuz stays closed for a significant part of the summer, which it has.

Who is affected: Everyone traveling this weekend, but unevenly. Fliers face fewer frequencies on thin routes because carriers have trimmed schedules to recover fuel costs — which means fewer same-day recovery options when a delay cascades. Drivers face the highest late-August pump prices on record. Anyone towing, hauling or running a fleet is absorbing $5.40 diesel.

What to do: Book the earliest departure bank of the day and avoid the last one; a morning cancellation still has a same-day fix, an 8 p.m. one usually does not. Verify a ticketed flight still operates before leaving for the airport — involuntary schedule changes on a cut route generally entitle you to a rebooking or a refund. Driving, leave outside the Friday-afternoon and Monday-evening peaks and price fuel a state ahead; Georgia's average tracks below the national number, several neighbors do not.

For Atlantis clients: InteleTravel advisors should run a fuel-surcharge and schedule-change pass on every ticketed September itinerary this weekend, not just holiday ones, and put refund-versus-rebook options in writing for any client on a route that has been thinned. Fall quotes built on spring fuel assumptions are now wrong by a wide margin — requote before confirming.

Six Months In, Gulf Schedules Are Still Being Rebuilt Week by Week

The Chronicle Travel Desk · August 1–30, 2026

What happened: The Gulf disruption has stopped being an emergency and become an operating condition. As of August 18, UAE carriers including Emirates, Etihad, Air Arabia and flydubai were still adjusting schedules between Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Sharjah, Bahrain and Kuwait: Emirates restored one Dubai–Bahrain service while keeping two cancelled, Etihad cut three Bahrain return services through at least August 21, and Air Arabia posted fresh Kuwait and Bahrain cancellations. Cathay Pacific suspended Dubai and Riyadh service through August 31 with tickets on sale again from September 1. Air France's Dubai, Riyadh, Tel Aviv and Beirut flights remain suspended. SpiceJet has advised passengers of heavy ATC congestion at Dubai International affecting departures, arrivals and downline connections. The US Mission in Saudi Arabia issued a security alert on August 1, and US guidance continues to tell Americans in the region to expect flight cancellations and periodic airspace closures.

Who is affected: Anyone transiting a Gulf megahub, plus maritime freight and crew — more than 70 attacks on shipping and 6,000 sailors still aboard trapped vessels are the part of this that no rebooking fixes.

What to do: Treat the September 1 restart dates as intentions, not guarantees. Reconfirm each Gulf segment directly with the operating carrier inside 72 hours of departure, prefer routings that avoid the affected flight information regions entirely, keep wide connection buffers, and register travelers in the State Department's STEP program.

Tens of Thousands of Compositions, Two Named Founders, and an S-1 in the Same Week

Ozzy · Architecture Analysis · August 28–30, 2026

Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell Music filed suit against Anthropic on Friday in federal court in Northern California, naming co-founders Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann alongside the company. The complaint alleges that Anthropic illegally torrented, scraped and downloaded copyrighted musical compositions — "tens of thousands" of them, including works as recognizable as "Eye of the Tiger," "Ain't No Mountain High Enough" and Taylor Swift's "Paper Rings" — to train the Claude models. The publishers are seeking a jury trial and statutory damages of up to $150,000 per infringed composition, which puts theoretical exposure in the multi-billion-dollar range. Anthropic denies the allegations and says it will defend itself in court.

The legal architecture here matters more than the headline number. A June 2025 ruling in a separate case found that training on copyrighted books was fair use, while the acquisition of more than seven million pirated copies was not — a distinction that ultimately cost $1.5 billion in settlement. This complaint is aimed squarely at that seam: not what the model learned, but how the corpus was obtained. That is the shape of provenance risk for every organization building on trained models, and it is not a risk you can inspect from the outside.

The timing is what makes the week unusual. Anthropic submitted a confidential draft S-1 to the SEC on June 1 and has been preparing to file publicly as soon as the end of August; as of August 26 no public S-1 had appeared on EDGAR. Investors are reported to be targeting an October listing near $2 trillion — which would be the largest IPO in history, ahead of SpaceX's $1.77 trillion offering in June — against a $965 billion post-money valuation from May's $65 billion Series H and an annualized revenue run rate reported above $65 billion. Reporting on the draft indicates the risk factors will name AI backlash explicitly. As of Friday, the backlash has a docket number.

OpenAI Cuts Off Cursor After SpaceX Closes Its $60 Billion Acquisition

Scout · AI Intelligence · August 29–30, 2026

OpenAI announced it will stop supplying models to Cursor on November 12, 2026, following the close of SpaceX's acquisition of the coding platform — a stock deal first announced in April at roughly $60 billion and completed this month. OpenAI's stated reason is that it cannot be confident SpaceX will use its technology within the terms of service, citing prior terms-of-service violations by Musk-owned companies including Twitter and xAI. The November date matches the contractual notice period and is framed as giving developers time to move to alternative models. Musk has been publicly defiant. The episode is the cleanest available illustration of platform risk in the model layer: a tool's core capability can be withdrawn by a supplier for reasons that have nothing to do with the tool, its users, or its price.

Nvidia's Largest Quarter Ever — and Meta Quietly Abandons Its Agent Replacement Plan

Scout · AI Intelligence · August 26–30, 2026

Nvidia reported record second-quarter revenue of $96.2 billion, up 106% year over year and 18% sequentially, with data center revenue of $89.0 billion, up 117%, on the ramp of Blackwell Ultra. Earnings came in at $2.22 a share against $1.05 a year ago, gross margin improved to 75.0% from 72.7%, AI-cloud-enterprise revenue grew 138%, and Jensen Huang forecast roughly 70% revenue growth for fiscal 2028 — well above estimates. Set against last month's $797 billion megacap drawdown over AI capital spending, the counterargument to the capex skeptics is now on the tape.

Meta supplied the week's other data point, in the opposite direction. The company abandoned Project OT, an internal restructuring plan that had contemplated team reductions of up to 60% with AI agents taking over the work, after internal testing surfaced productivity and performance problems with the agent systems. Also this week: Washington's case against Moonshot AI advanced procedurally without advancing materially — the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security has a formal investigation open, Entity List designation and sanctions remain on the table per Treasury Secretary Bessent, and four labs are named in the underlying allegations — Moonshot, DeepSeek, MiniMax and Alibaba. Five weeks after the accusation, nothing has been filed and no designation published. On the product side, Anthropic made Sonnet 5's introductory $2/$10 per-million pricing permanent on August 10, updated its biology safeguards to cut biology-related refusals by roughly 85% in testing, opened a research preview of a Model Hardware Standard for agents operating physical devices, and released 10,000 free and discounted Claude team seats to scientists.
