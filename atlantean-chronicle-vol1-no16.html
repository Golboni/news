A Missile Hits Riyadh's Airport, and the Iran War Opens a Southern Front

A missile struck the domestic terminals at King Khalid International Airport on Saturday, killing at least 12 people — one of them an American — and wounding more than 300, the third attack on the airport that week. Saudi Arabia's main gateway is closed, Brent is above $104, and President Trump says Washington is weighing whether to join the Saudi campaign against the Houthis. Seven months into the war, the conflict that began at the Strait of Hormuz now reaches the Arabian Peninsula's busiest airport.
By The Chronicle International Desk · October 9–11, 2026

The deadliest strike yet on the Saudi capital came on Saturday, October 10. A missile hit the complex housing Terminals 3 and 4 at King Khalid International Airport, which handle domestic flights, according to a regional official. Saudi Arabia's civil aviation authority put the toll at no fewer than 12 dead and 309 injured, several in critical condition. The dead included four Saudis, two Bangladeshis, and one each from the United States, Jordan, Palestine, Syria, Sudan and Egypt; Secretary of State Marco Rubio confirmed an American was among them. Reporting described it as the deadliest attack in any Gulf Arab country since the Iran war began. The Saudi-led coalition in Yemen blamed the Houthis, and by Sunday evening the group had claimed responsibility for Saturday's strike and for several further attacks on the airport.

It was the third attack on the airport in a week. On Thursday, October 8, Houthi strikes killed three Saudis, including an airline pilot, and damaged three aircraft; operations resumed Friday. Saturday's strike came after the airport had reopened. On Sunday, Saudi authorities extended the closure, at least 291 flights had been cancelled in 24 hours, and a fresh attack was reported on Dammam airport that evening. The Houthis also claimed attacks on Saudi energy facilities. An Indian national injured Saturday later died, and Saudi aviation authorities reported a Pakistani resident killed in a further strike, so the toll was still rising as the weekend ended.

The diplomatic reaction was broad and fast. President Trump called the attack "terrible," said he would speak soon with Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, and said the United States is evaluating whether to join the war against the Houthis — "We may. We're gonna look at it," he said, adding that a decision would come quickly. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he was "deeply shocked," and UN envoy Hans Grundberg called strikes on civilian airports "a grave escalation." Qatar called it a serious violation of Saudi sovereignty; Jordan, Egypt, Turkey, Pakistan, India, Japan and Canada condemned it; Oman urged restraint. Australia's foreign minister told Australians to reconsider travel to Saudi Arabia. Saudi and Iranian foreign ministers spoke on Sunday, and Tehran offered to help facilitate Saudi–Yemen dialogue.

Markets noticed. Brent settled Friday at $104.72, up 0.4%, and reached $105.48 in Sunday-evening trade with WTI near $92.44, on the combined weight of the airport strikes and a tanker struck by a drone near the Sohar anchorage off Oman. Saudi Arabia is already running an air campaign against the Houthis in support of Yemeni government forces, and Saturday's toll came as Yemen's war was intensifying.

The war's center of gravity keeps moving: first the strait, then the tankers, now the departure hall of a Gulf capital. For operators, the practical point is that the region's aviation hubs are exposed from two directions at once — the strait to the east and Yemen to the southwest — and that a reopening announcement from one authority is no guarantee of a flight.

Hormuz Recovered in September. October Is Taking It Back.

The Chronicle International Desk · October 5–11, 2026

No. 15's Oracle priced a return to normal Hormuz traffic before 2027 at 45%. September delivered much of it. After President Trump declared the strait cleared of mines in late August, Gulf flows excluding Iran recovered to more than 81% of pre-war levels, against a pre-war baseline of roughly 125 large commercial vessels a day carrying about a fifth of the world's crude and LNG, and Middle East crude exports exceeded pre-war levels on 14 separate days.

October has reversed much of that. The tracking firm Kpler counted 10 tankers struck between September 28 and October 4, with another on October 5. An Iranian official told Reuters in early October that Tehran had changed the type and pace of its attacks, linking the shift to influencing the November US midterms. On Sunday the US-led Joint Maritime Information Center kept the threat level at "severe," citing IRGC attacks, harassment, drone overflights and radio hailing of vessels, and a tanker was struck by a drone off Oman. That night Iranian security chief Mohsen Rezaei claimed Iran has been hitting five or six vessels nightly for weeks, calling them part of a US–UAE shuttle system; the claim is unverified.

On the diplomatic track, Iran says Washington accepted some of its seven conditions for a deal but calls the overall US reply "insufficient." The US naval blockade of Iranian ports continues, and CENTCOM maintains that Iran does not control the strait.

Ukraine: A Diesel Deal, a Truce Announced by Post, and a Strike Within Hours

The Chronicle International Desk · October 9–11, 2026

Ukraine. On Friday, after speaking with Vladimir Putin days earlier, President Trump announced a plan to supply markets with Russian diesel; terms were not detailed. Envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner were in talks in Miami the same day. On Saturday Trump said Ukraine should "get a new leader who can make a deal," according to reports. On Sunday morning he posted that an energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine was in effect "immediately" and that both sides had agreed — ending, "Do not break it!" President Zelensky told Axios the post was the first he had heard of any such deal, that Kyiv awaited details from Washington, and that Ukraine would agree if Russia did. Moscow had not commented. Within hours, Russia reportedly struck a combined heat-and-power plant near Kharkiv, after weeks of strikes on Ukraine's energy grid, telecoms and data centers ahead of winter. A similar mid-September truce announcement was never confirmed by either side.

Nobel Peace Prize. The Norwegian Nobel Committee announced Friday in Oslo that the 2026 Peace Prize goes to Navanethem "Navi" Pillay, the South African jurist and former UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, for her "efforts to promote peace and international law." Committee chair Jørgen Watne Frydnes framed the award as a call to strengthen international law. The ceremony is December 10.

The Fed Hiked. The Bond Market Wants More Convincing.

The Chronicle National Desk · October 5–9, 2026

No. 15 put a September hike near a coin flip. It happened. On September 16 the Federal Open Market Committee raised the federal funds target by a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00%, its first increase since July 2023. Chair Warsh described the move as removing accommodation rather than tightening policy, and the median projection pointed to one more hike in 2026 and none in 2027.

Long-term rates have not settled. A global bond selloff pushed the 10-year Treasury yield above 5.3% at the start of October — its highest level in more than 24 years — and it finished Friday at 5.24% after several swings through the session. The 30-year has traded above 5.6%, the 10-year is up more than a full percentage point this year, and the Bloomberg US Aggregate Index has turned negative for 2026. Coverage ties the move to energy-driven inflation, the Fed's rate path and heavy government borrowing.

Equities are telling a different story. The S&P 500 closed Friday at 7,811.54, up 0.6% and within reach of the record it set earlier in the week; it gained 1.2% on the week, the Dow 0.9% and the Nasdaq 0.6%. Small caps, the segment most sensitive to financing costs, fell 0.9% on the week. The next test arrives Wednesday with September consumer prices, and the Fed meets again October 27–28.

The Shutdown Clock Ran Out Quietly — and the Midterms Are 23 Days Away

The Chronicle National Desk · October 9–11, 2026

The 30-day countdown flagged in No. 15 ended without drama. A stopgap funding bill running through December 11 at current spending levels was signed on September 2, after passing the Senate 90–6 and the House 370–48, so October 1 passed with the government open. The cost is a new cliff: the next funding deadline now lands in the lame-duck session after the November 3 midterms.

Those elections now organize most of the week's news. Prediction markets give Democrats about a 92% chance of winning the House and roughly 65% on the Senate (see Oracle). Early voting opens in Georgia on Tuesday. Energy is the political variable everyone is pricing: an Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran changed its attacks with the midterms in mind, and the Russian diesel plan Trump announced Friday followed pressure on Ukraine to stop hitting Russian refineries, a link the president has drawn to rising diesel prices. AAA's national gasoline average stood at $4.41 on October 1 — $2.98 before the war — and diesel set a record of $6.29 in late September.

Also this week: The Nobel Peace Prize went to Navi Pillay (see International). Trump said Washington is evaluating whether to join Saudi strikes on the Houthis, a decision he said would come quickly.

Riyadh's Main Airport Is Closed — What Travelers Need to Know

The Chronicle Travel Desk · October 8–11, 2026

What happened: King Khalid International Airport was hit on Thursday, October 8, in an attack that killed three Saudis, including a pilot, and damaged three aircraft; it reopened Friday. On Saturday a missile struck the domestic terminal complex, killing at least 12 and injuring 309, and operations were suspended again. Saudi authorities extended the closure on Sunday with no reopening date, at least 291 flights were cancelled in 24 hours, and a further attack was reported on Dammam airport Sunday evening. The EU aviation safety agency expanded its conflict-zone advisory on Friday as a safety recommendation rather than an airspace closure, and the UK, US, Canada, Ireland and Germany told citizens to avoid the airport and nearby areas.

Who is affected: Anyone with a Riyadh itinerary or a Riyadh connection, plus travelers through Dammam and other Saudi airports. As of Sunday, Air India had cancelled Riyadh service through October 15 and Air India Express through October 14; flydubai, Etihad, Qatar Airways, Kuwait Airways and Lufthansa had cancelled or suspended Riyadh flights with no end dates, and schedules were changing at short notice.

What to do: Do not go to the airport for a Saudi flight unless your airline confirms it is operating. Check status with the operating carrier, not a booking site, and again inside 24 hours. Ask for a refund or a rebooking through an alternative hub in writing; keep receipts for hotels and meals. Register with the State Department's STEP program, and contact your embassy if stranded. If the trip is discretionary, postpone it.

For Atlantis clients: Advisors should pull every itinerary touching Riyadh, Dammam, Jeddah or a Gulf connection this weekend, confirm carrier status directly, and put refund-versus-reroute options in writing before the client calls. Quote nothing in Saudi Arabia for October without a stated cancellation path.

The Timeline Moves Right: What Is Reported, and What Anthropic Has Confirmed

Ozzy · Architecture Analysis · October 9–11, 2026

Anthropic announced on June 1 that it had confidentially submitted a draft S-1 to the SEC; its last confirmed private valuation is the $965 billion post-money figure from May's $65 billion Series H. A confidential submission is not visible on EDGAR, and a public filing has not followed. Reuters reported in September that the expected window had slipped from "after Labor Day" to "late September." The Wall Street Journal and Reuters have both reported that a November listing is possible, and one outlet reports an investor event at Anthropic's San Francisco headquarters on October 14; the company declined to comment on that report.

The arithmetic matters. A public S-1 must be on EDGAR at least 15 days before a roadshow begins, so a roadshow the week of November 9 would put the practical filing date near October 23. One report puts a first trading day on November 26, which is Thanksgiving Day, when US exchanges are closed — a sign the reported dates are approximate rather than fixed. Discussed valuations run from roughly $1.8 trillion to $3 trillion, the New York Times has reported a raise that could exceed $100 billion, and Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Citigroup are reported as lead banks. None of this is confirmed by the company, and neither the exchange nor the price range has been announced.

The litigation backdrop has not changed. Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell sued on August 28, naming co-founders Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann and seeking up to $150,000 per work; reporting describes related music-publisher cases from BMG and Round Hill. No ruling in the Sony/Warner case was located. A missing S-1 is consistent with SEC comments, market conditions or litigation disclosure; nothing public says which.

Nvidia Buys Hugging Face; OpenAI Opens a Math Archive; Four Labs Decline a Guarantee

Ozzy · AI Intelligence · September 2–October 8, 2026

Nvidia signed a definitive agreement on September 2 to acquire Hugging Face for about $12.93 billion — roughly $11.9 billion to shareholders plus up to about $1.0 billion in retention stock — with closing expected in the first half of 2027 pending regulatory approvals. US review would run through FTC and DOJ and the EU would open a Phase I review; the open question is whether the chip leader's ownership of the model hub nudges developers toward CUDA-optimized models. The company's own filing flags risks from government restrictions on open-source models.

On October 6, OpenAI published a public repository of 719 mathematics manuscripts across 372 problem families written by a model it has not released. The same day, at a New York City Council hearing, representatives of OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and Google declined to guarantee that their AI agents will always follow safety guardrails. On October 8, Google published a Lancet trial in which its AMIE system matched physicians on diagnosis quality with 100 real patients. And on Moonshot AI, the Commerce Department's investigation remains open with Entity List designation "on the table"; no designation was located as of Sunday.
